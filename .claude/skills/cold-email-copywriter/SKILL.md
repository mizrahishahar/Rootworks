# Cold email copywriter

A cold email has one job: earn a reply from a stranger who did not ask to hear from us and will give it five seconds. The reply is earned by the offer and by specificity, not by prose. Words get varied heavily later; what must be right first is the argument.

Everything here is craft. It holds whatever sender, database or tool is under it. How a token renders, where a number is stored, how a row reaches a campaign: those belong to the tool's own skill, and this one names them only by role.

## The laws

**1. Consistency.** The whole email argues one thing. The offer to this reader, the outcome, the mechanism, the proof, the risk reversal, the CTA: one argument, no seams. Every variant carries the same argument in a different angle; the follow-up collects on the same outcome; every spintax option says the same thing. The reader cannot name a seam, but feels it, and trust dies there.

The classic break and the fix:

> Broken: "We can get you 25 more sales, just like we helped XY respond 50% faster."
> Outcome A, proof of B.
>
> Fixed: "We can get you more sales BY making you respond 50% faster, just like we did for XY."

The bridge is BY. When the outcome you want and the proof you hold do not match, never stretch the proof: connect them, outcome BY mechanism, proof of the mechanism. No honest BY means the email cannot make that promise. Run the seam check on every draft: read each part alone and ask whether it is about the same one thing.

**2. The offer carries most of it.** A plain email on a strong offer beats a beautiful email on a weak one. Grade the offer before writing a sentence (`offer.md`). A weak offer is flagged, never dressed.

**3. Specificity is the proof of research.** The line that earns the read is one a person could only have written after looking: the review by name, the days since their last post, the tier names on their pricing page, the ad they are running. The standard: research this company for ten minutes, then say what you would say after that. "I loved your website", "I saw you are the CEO", "I saw you sell to fitness coaches" fail it; generic personalization reads worse than none. And the research is never announced: state the fact, never that you found it.

**4. Nothing invented.** No client, number, result or asset the knowledge base did not give. A number is used verbatim only when its source is verified; two sources disagreeing means stop and ask, never average. An asset promised must exist.

**5. A real person wrote it.** Nothing you would not say out loud to one specific person. No links in the body, no bold, no marketing cadence, no "solutions". If a line could sit on a homepage, it is wrong. The read-aloud test decides every sentence.

**6. Written to the row.** Copy is written to what each reader's row carries. A variable is a fact on the row with a fallback, so the worst render is still a clean sentence. Copy that leans on a fact the list does not hold is a flag, not a wish.

## What you must know before writing

Not where to get it, but what you must be able to answer. Missing means say so, never guess.

| About | The questions |
|---|---|
| The reader | Who exactly, at what kind of company, selected on what. What facts each row carries. In which language and register they talk |
| The offer | The real terms: the outcome, the mechanism, what is done for them, the guarantee or pay-per-result, the price model. Which proof exists and which numbers are verified |
| The history | What already went to these readers or this client, which angle, and what it produced |
| The constraints | How this client differs from the default, banned words, legal limits, who signs the email |

## The shape before the words

Before writing bodies, show the shape and get a yes. It costs two minutes and prevents a sequence written in the wrong direction.

| Line | What it says |
|---|---|
| Reader | who, from which list |
| Playbook | the template, and per variant the opener it runs on |
| Outcome | the one result, in the reader's units, with its number when honest |
| Mechanism | the how, in one clause |
| Proof | which result, and how close it is to this reader |
| Risk reversal | the guarantee or the free slice, in its sharpest true words |
| CTA | what the yes buys, and the words |
| Variables | each token, the fact it carries, its fallback |
| Follow-up | what the one follow-up adds |

**One campaign or several.** Split where the argument changes, never where the data changes. A CFO at 200 people and a founder at 15 hear different outcomes from the same offer: two campaigns. Two groups that differ only in vocabulary or a variable: one campaign. Variants test angles at one reader; they never serve two readers.

**Playbooks are templates.** Each one in `playbooks/` is a description, a template and examples, nothing more. Choose on what we actually hold for this reader (a signal, a matched proof, a free deliverable, a built thing, an asset), never on what sounds best. A different playbook is a different campaign, never a variant.

## The anatomy

Every good cold email answers six questions. Not every email answers all six; the ones it answers, it answers sharply.

1. Why them
2. Why now
3. What relevant proof do we show
4. Why would they believe we actually looked
5. What is their risk in saying yes, and how is it reversed
6. What do they get for saying yes, and is the CTA worth taking on its own

The parts, and where each one's leverage is:

**The opener.** A fact that belongs to this reader alone, a sharp question about their world, or the whole offer stated plainly. Its leverage is specificity. Category talk, fake familiarity and benefit lines are the opposite of an opener.

**The outcome.** One result, in their units, quantified when the knowledge base allows. "10 to 20 sales calls a month" beats "more leads". Two outcomes in one email means neither is argued. Choose the outcome for the segment: money saved to one reader, money made to another.

**The mechanism.** How the outcome happens, in one clause, and ideally something they have not seen before. Unique and clear together: when the reader can see how it would work, belief is their own conclusion and nothing had to be swallowed.

**The proof.** The result closest to this reader: same trade, same size, same city. Carried by numbers, as a delta ("$2M to $7M, 13 trucks to 51"). A proof the reader would not recognise glazes eyes; describe the outcome and the kind of company, and name the company only when this reader would know it. A strong offer can carry an email with no proof at all.

**The risk reversal.** What happens if we do not deliver, in the sharpest words the real terms allow: "half your inquiries solved from day one, or you pay nothing". Phrased never wider than its conditions. It matters most when proof is thin; an email with neither is asking a stranger for faith. Honest scarcity is its sibling: true, checkable, and it explains why them.

**The CTA.** Value plus an easy yes, together. The yes costs one word; what it buys is worth something on its own, even if the sale never happens. Value-shaped asks (a version built for them, the breakdown, the intro, the sample) beat time asks; "if we could help you with this, would it be useful?" beats "want to book a call". The free thing must pull them closer, never satisfy from a distance: a free audit that lands by email produces yeses and no meetings. When the offer already carries the heavy value (a real guarantee, a free first slice), the CTA can be light; when the offer is thin, the CTA has to compensate.

**The P.S.** The second most read line. One of two jobs, never both: a second, different value proposition (the body argues money made, the P.S. argues money saved), or the door out ("not relevant? just reply no thanks", which generates real replies from people who would otherwise ignore or flag). A second signal also lives well here: if the premise was wrong about Karen's review, name Brian's.

**Length.** 50 to 90 words. Shorter loses the room to be believed; longer loses the read.

**The subject.** Two to four words a colleague, a customer or a peer would send: "pizza orders", "sales reports", "{{first_name}} <> Sean". The body must deliver on it, no bait and switch. Personalized subjects stay lowercase. No punctuation tricks, no emoji, never "curious".

## Personalization and variables

Ask first: does the reader's context change how the offer helps them? When the pitch is the same whatever they do (a vacuum cleans every floor the same way), keep the copy static and lean on the situation (a hire, a signal, a gap). When the context changes the pitch (an agency serves a hospital and a hotel differently), a researched variable earns its place.

Levels, from strongest to weakest:

| Level | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A signal | something they did or have, named plainly, spent once | "saw the review Karen left about the late crew" |
| A specificity line | how we would help this company in particular, from their own site, six to fourteen words | "help you know the landed cost of every pool float before you price it" |
| A situation line | one true sentence about their setup | "one person carrying infra right now" |
| The whole offer | no personalization, the offer self-selects | "8 booked calls in 60 days or you don't pay" |

Every variable is declared with the fact it carries and a fallback that still reads as a sentence. A variable that could be wrong about the reader is worse than none. Never nest a variable inside a spun slot.

## The follow-up

One follow-up. Never two. It threads under the first email, and it exists because a non-reply means the first angle did not land: it opens with a reason to be there (a held-back detail, a second proof, a true deadline, the same offer arguing a different outcome), and re-asks. Shorter than the first email. Never "following up", "circling back", "checking in", never a reference to the earlier email. After it, the reader is left alone; a return comes months later, with a new campaign, when their world may have changed.

## Rules

- Openers never: "most [industry] struggle with", "if you're like most", "are you tired of", "imagine if", "did you know", "I hope this finds you well", "I came across", "quick question" with no question.
- The door-out line promises to stop on an explicit reply, never on silence. "A one-word no is enough and I'll step out of your inbox", never "I'll take silence as a no".
- No em dashes. No links, bold or images in the body. No greeting before the name in the subject.
- The company is named the way they name it, cleaned; the reader's first name in their own language.
- Speak to the person: "you", never "[industry] companies". Match the words to the title: trucks and crews to the owner, net revenue to the CFO, the stack to the engineer.
- Every number verbatim from a verified source; every asset real.

## The read before showing

Run on every email, silently, and rewrite before showing, never footnote:

1. One argument, seam check passed
2. Opener is a fact this reader owns, a real question, or the whole offer
3. One outcome, in their units, with its number when honest
4. Proof matched to the reader, unnamed unless recognised
5. Risk reversal in its sharpest true words, or a strong enough offer without it
6. CTA worth taking alone, a one-word yes
7. 50 to 90 words, reads aloud like a person
8. Every variable declared with a fallback; nothing the list does not hold
9. The follow-up threaded, adding something, never re-introducing
10. Rules above, all of them

## How copy is shown

- **Shape first**, as the table above. Words only after the yes.
- Then the sequence, every variant, both touches. One heading per touch. The variant line carries the letter, the angle in two words, and the subject, `·`-separated. Body as a blockquote. `---` between touches, never between variants.
- **Never print the same body twice.** Variants sharing a body and differing by subject stack their lines above the one body.
- Clean copy: real tokens as the row carries them, no signature, no spintax inline. Spintax is its own artifact (`spintax.md`).
- **Reprint the whole sequence on every refinement.** A line that reads well alone can break the email around it.
- Options for a line are tabled inside their full sentence, with a one-line read and a marked recommendation. After a revision, a short table of what changed and why.
- Tables are real markdown tables, never inside code fences.

```
### Touch 1

**Variant A**  ·  *signal*  ·  *Subject:* net revenue at {{company_name}}

> Hey {{first_name}},
>
> the body.

---

### Touch 2

*Threaded, +3 days · single variant*

> the body.
```

## After the yes

An approved sequence becomes a campaign by the order of acts in `launching.md`: built as a draft on the sender, read back, given its Hub row and its copy, bound to a segment with Stage Test. What the campaign must carry on any sender, its stages and its lines, is the standard [[campaigns]]; nothing here restates it.

## What this skill flags on its own

- The offer is not a cold offer: no economic outcome, no risk reversal, nothing they could not get from a search. Named as an offer problem, not written around.
- The angle leans on a fact the list does not carry.
- The proof does not match the reader, or the only proof is a name they would not know.
- This angle already ran to these readers and did not land.
- A seam between outcome, mechanism, proof and CTA that no honest BY closes.
- Personalization that announces the research, or that could be wrong about the reader.
- A free thing in the CTA that satisfies without pulling closer.
- Two campaigns hiding in one: a reader who would hear a different outcome.
