Teaches: the parts of a cold email, the biggest leverage of each, and the consistency law that binds them into one argument.

# The parts, and where each one's leverage is

A cold email is a few sentences, each doing one of these jobs. Not every email carries every part: the playbook decides which parts this campaign runs, and some of the best emails are three parts total. What is always true: know which job a sentence is doing, and you know what would make it stronger. A sentence doing no job is a cut.

## Opener

The first line, read on a phone notification next to the subject. Its only job: prove this email is about THEM, not about a category, in one breath.

**Biggest leverage: a specific, true observation the reader owns.** A number they recognize ("rated 3.7 on Trustpilot"), a situation they are living ("one person carrying infra"), a place they exist ("kept coming up when I looked up movers in Austin"). The more the fact belongs to them alone, the more the email earns its read. Never open with category talk ("most brands struggle with..."), fake familiarity ("came across your profile"), or a benefit line.

## Outcome

The one concrete result this email promises. Not a capability, not a feature list, a result: more booked moves, half the inquiries solved, $200K a year not spent.

		**Biggest leverage: favour the outcome over everything else.** One outcome per email, quantified when the KB allows, stated in the reader's units (moves, tickets, calls, shekels), and everything else in the email exists to make that one outcome believable. Two outcomes in one email means neither is argued properly.

**The outcome is chosen for the segment.** The same offer carries different outcomes to different readers: saving money and generating money are different arguments, and a CFO at 200 people hears the first where a founder at 15 hears the second. Which audiences exist, and which outcome each one gets, is a decision made with the list (list-builder owns the segmentation); the copy's job is to carry the outcome its segment was cut for, and to say something a neighbouring segment's copy could not. If the copy could be sent to the segment next door unchanged, either the segmentation or the outcome choice is wrong, and that is worth raising before writing on.

## Mechanism

How the outcome happens. One clause, usually: "an AI agent that plugs into your systems", "leads, quoting, and dispatch on one board".

**Biggest leverage: just enough to make the outcome plausible, never enough to explain the product.** The mechanism answers "how could that be true?" and stops. Giving the mechanism away plainly also does the honesty work: the reader can judge for themselves, which is exactly the non-needy posture.

## Proof

The evidence: a named client, a real number, a story compressed to one line.

**Biggest leverage: proximity.** The proof closest to who is reading wins: same country, same size, same problem. A roofing result sells roofers. Second lever: shape it as a story with a delta ("$2M to $7M, 13 trucks to 51"), because a delta is felt where a state is not.

## Risk reversal

The line that moves the risk off the reader: the guarantee, the free-to-try, the performance term.

**Biggest leverage: phrase it in the sharpest cold-facing words the actual terms allow.** "Half your inquiries solved from day one, or you pay nothing" beats "results guaranteed". Never soften it, never bury it, and never phrase a guarantee wider than its real conditions. Honest scarcity ("we only offer this to brands in your range") is risk reversal's sibling: true, checkable, and it explains WHY them.

## CTA

The ask. One, small, value-shaped.

**Biggest leverage: make it feel already done, and make it cost one word to accept.** "I can build you a first version right here over email. Want me to?" beats every "worth a quick call?". The reader should be saying yes to receiving something, not to giving something. Friction on the CTA is a dial: a hot list with strong proof can carry an intro ask, a cold saturated one wants the lightest possible yes.

# The consistency law

**Everything in the email talks about one thing.** The offer to the persona, the mechanism, the proof, the risk reversal, the CTA: one argument, one outcome, no seams.

The classic break, and the fix:

> **Broken:** "We can get you 25 more sales, just like we helped XY respond 50% faster to clients."
>
> The outcome is A (more sales). The proof proves B (faster response). The reader feels the seam even when they cannot name it, and trust dies at the seam.

> **Fixed:** "We can get you more sales BY making you respond 50% faster, just like we did for XY."
>
> One argument. The proof now proves the mechanism, and the mechanism delivers the outcome. Same facts, zero seams.

**The bridge is the word BY.** When the outcome you want to promise and the proof you actually have do not match, never stretch the proof. Connect them: outcome BY mechanism, proof of the mechanism. If no honest BY exists between them, the email cannot make that promise; use the outcome the proof supports, or raise the gap as an offer problem.

Run the seam check on every draft: read outcome, mechanism, proof, risk reversal, CTA in isolation and ask if all five are about the same one thing. Any part about a different thing is the part to rewrite.
