Teaches: the parts a cold email can run, the biggest leverage of each, and the consistency law that binds them into one argument.

# The parts, and where each one's leverage is

A cold email is a few sentences, each doing one of these jobs. Not every email carries every part: the playbook decides which parts this campaign runs, and some of the best emails are three parts total. What is always true: know which job a sentence is doing, and you know what would make it stronger. A sentence doing no job is a cut.

## The observation

The thing that hooks them in, used wherever the playbook uses it. It can be a stated fact, a question, or an assumption; what makes it work is never the form, it is the specificity.

**Biggest leverage: specific and theirs.** A number they recognize ("rated 3.7 on Trustpilot"), a detail of their situation ("one person carrying infra"), a genuinely relevant pain named sharply, a question about their world they can answer in one line, an assumption about how they operate that is close enough to sting. The more the observation belongs to this reader alone, the more the email earns its read. Category talk ("most brands struggle with..."), fake familiarity, and benefit lines are the opposite of an observation.

## Outcome

The one concrete result this email promises: more booked moves, half the inquiries solved, $200K a year not spent.

**Biggest leverage: favour the outcome over everything else, and put a number on it wherever possible.** One outcome per email, in the reader's units (moves, tickets, calls, shekels), quantified whenever the KB allows. "10 to 20 sales calls a month" beats "more leads" every time it can be honestly said. Everything else in the email exists to make that one outcome believable; two outcomes in one email means neither is argued properly.

**The outcome is chosen for the segment.** The same offer carries different outcomes to different readers: saving money and generating money are different arguments, and a CFO at 200 people hears the first where a founder at 15 hears the second. The copy's job is to carry the outcome its segment was cut for, and to say something a neighbouring segment's copy could not. If the copy could be sent to the segment next door unchanged, either the segmentation or the outcome choice is wrong, and that is worth raising before writing on. Deciding where the audience splits at all is `scoping.md`.

## Mechanism

How the outcome happens, in one clause: "an AI agent that plugs into your systems", "leads, quoting, and dispatch on one board".

**Biggest leverage: unique AND clear, together.** Unique means something they have never seen before; clear means understood in one read. They are not in tension, and the best mechanisms are both. When a mechanism is unique and clear, the reader starts believing on their own, because they can see for themselves how the outcome would happen; no claim had to be swallowed. That is the whole point: not to dress something up, but to hand over the real how so plainly that belief is the reader's own conclusion. A mechanism that needs jargon to sound impressive is hiding something, and the reader smells it.

## Proof

The evidence: a named client, a real number, a story compressed to one line.

**Biggest leverage: proximity, carried by numbers.** The proof closest to who is reading wins: same country, same size, same problem. A roofing result sells roofers. And wherever the KB allows, the proof carries its numbers: a delta is felt where a state is not ("$2M to $7M, 13 trucks to 51" beats "grew significantly" by a mile). A proof without a number is a testimonial; a proof with one is evidence.

## Risk reversal

The line that answers the question every cold reader is silently asking: **what happens if you do not deliver the outcome, and how little can I risk to find out?** The guarantee, the free-to-try, the pay-per-result term, the free first slice.

**Biggest leverage: phrase it in the sharpest cold-facing words the actual terms allow.** "Half your inquiries solved from day one, or you pay nothing" beats "results guaranteed". Never soften it, never bury it, never phrase it wider than its real conditions.

**It becomes crucial when proof is thin.** A strong matched proof lets the risk reversal be light; with no proof, the risk reversal has to carry the believability alone, and having it, and having it right, decides the email. The two trade against each other: the reader needs SOME reason the downside is covered, from evidence or from terms, and an email with neither is asking a stranger for faith. Honest scarcity ("we only offer this to brands in your range") is risk reversal's sibling: true, checkable, and it explains WHY them.

## CTA

The ask that closes the email.

**Biggest leverage: value plus an easy yes, together.** The point is never to offer the most impressive thing; it is the combination of simple and valuable. The yes should cost one word, and what the yes buys should be worth something. It does not have to be instant; sometimes the valuable thing takes days to deliver, and that is fine as long as saying yes stayed easy.

**The offer decides how much the CTA must carry.** The hierarchy: free value embedded in the offer itself is the strongest position, better than value carried by the CTA. When the offer already contains the massive thing (a real risk reversal, a free first slice built into the terms), the CTA gets to be simple: more info, a text breakdown, a loom, a walkthrough, and that simplicity is the preferred move. When the offer is meh, the CTA has to compensate: think about what can be offered directly in the ask itself, a deliverable, something built for them. A weak offer with a plain "want more info?" is two weaknesses stacked.

## The P.S.

The second most-read line in the email, after the subject. A place too valuable to leave empty, used one of two ways:

- **The door out.** "not relevant? just reply 'no thanks'." Generates real replies from people who would otherwise ignore or spam-flag, and real replies are deliverability. This is its usual home in the second touch.
- **The spotlight.** The single most important piece of the email, given the one position where a skimmer will still see it: the bet, the guarantee, the number. One P.S., one job; a P.S. carrying two things carries nothing.

# The consistency law

**Everything in the email talks about one thing.** The offer to the persona, the mechanism, the proof, the risk reversal, the CTA: one argument, one outcome, no seams. The prospect is not dumb; a seam they cannot name is still a seam they feel, and trust dies at the seam.

The classic break, and the fix:

> **Broken:** "We can get you 25 more sales, just like we helped XY respond 50% faster to clients."
>
> The outcome is A (more sales). The proof proves B (faster response).

> **Fixed:** "We can get you more sales BY making you respond 50% faster, just like we did for XY."
>
> One argument. The proof now proves the mechanism, and the mechanism delivers the outcome. Same facts, zero seams.

**The bridge is the word BY.** When the outcome you want to promise and the proof you actually have do not match, never stretch the proof. Connect them: outcome BY mechanism, proof of the mechanism. If no honest BY exists between them, the email cannot make that promise; use the outcome the proof supports, or raise the gap as an offer problem.

Run the seam check on every draft: read observation, outcome, mechanism, proof, risk reversal, CTA in isolation and ask if every part present is about the same one thing. Any part about a different thing is the part to rewrite.
