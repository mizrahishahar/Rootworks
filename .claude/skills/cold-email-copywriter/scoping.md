Teaches: how to decide whether an audience is one campaign or several, by where the argument itself changes.

# Scoping the message

Before writing, one question decides the shape of everything: **does everyone in this audience get to hear the same thing, or would part of it be better off hearing something else?** The offer and the outcomes may already be good; scoping is noticing that a certain title, or a certain company profile, would respond to a different outcome, a different emphasis, or a different proof, and splitting the campaigns accordingly.

## What forces a split

A split exists where the ARGUMENT changes, not where the data changes. Two groups belong to two campaigns when any of these differ between them:

| Axis | The question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcome** | Would this profile respond to a different result from the same offer? | saving money to CFOs at scale, generating money to founders; efficiency to ops, growth to sales leaders |
| **Offer emphasis** | Does one group need a different pillar carried forward? | risk reversal leading for skeptical corporate buyers, the free slice leading for owner-operators |
| **Proof** | Does each group have a closer proof on hand? | the UK case to UK brands, the enterprise logo to enterprise, the peer story to peers |
| **Mechanism relevance** | Does the how land differently? | "plugs into Shopify" is the mechanism for one group and noise for another |
| **Language and altitude** | Does the same argument need different words and flying height? | trucks and crews to the owner, net revenue to the CFO, the stack to the engineer |

**The test, run both ways:**

- Two groups differ in outcome, offer emphasis, or proof: **two campaigns.** Variants cannot carry it; a variant tests angles at ONE audience, it does not serve two.
- Two groups differ only in vocabulary or a variable: **one campaign**, the difference carried by word choices or a personalization variable.
- Nothing in the argument changes between two groups: **one campaign**, and cutting them apart would just split the volume for nothing.

## Reading a company profile for the split

Title is one axis; the company itself is the other. Size, model, and maturity change what the same title cares about: a 15-person company's founder is the operator and hears the pain directly; a 200-person company's same-named title is a buyer with a budget and hears the business case. Vertical changes which proof is proximate and which words are native. When the profile shifts what the argument must be, that profile is its own campaign, whatever the title column says.

## The message map

Scoping's artifact, shown before any copy:

| Campaign | Who (title x profile) | Outcome they hear | Leading pillar | Proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| 2 | ... | ... | ... | ... |

One line under it saying what was deliberately NOT split and why. Two rows carrying the same outcome, pillar, and proof are one row. A row that names an audience no current list isolates is a real finding: it means the segmentation and the message do not match yet, and that gets said before anything sends.
