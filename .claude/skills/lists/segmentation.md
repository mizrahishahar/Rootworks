Teaches: whether a pool splits into more than one campaign, on what axis, in what priority, and the deliverability fence. The judgment of the split; expressing it as views is the clayroots skill.

# Segmentation

Segmentation is not a step done to a list. It is the campaign: how the pool is sliced decides how many campaigns exist, what copy each can truthfully say, and which variables have to be on every row. Done right, the copy writes itself, because the segment already made the message specific.

## Start from the brief, not the fields

The offer, the angle being tested, which campaigns already exist, what is to be learned. A segment set drawn from field values alone produces slices nobody can write copy for. If the brief does not say, ask before slicing. The count of campaigns is emergent, never preset.

## Two conditions, both required

**Size:** big enough to stand as a campaign and read a real signal; fold thin slices rather than starve a standalone one. **A reason:** the split lets the copy say something genuinely new to that audience. If the reason a segment exists cannot be said in one sentence, it does not exist.

## Segmentation over personalisation

- A native segment out-pulls broad personalisation: slice so one specific line is honest for the whole segment.
- The cheapest high-leverage field is the subcategory one level below the obvious category ("a roofing contractor", not "a contractor"), captured at sourcing so the opening line can be true.
- Persona call-outs land at larger firms (about 200 and up); subcategory call-outs land below that.
- Every variable is one of two things: changes a word, it personalises, a merge field inside one campaign; changes the angle, it marks a different audience, its own campaign.

## The two moments

Segments are designed twice, deliberately. At scoping, the segments a split already implies are set, which secures the variables. In the base, off the relevant-and-found pool, the full set is drawn once the real distribution is readable. Group by a candidate field and read it before proposing a split on it; a field with 80% blanks is not an axis.

## The axes

No fixed set; read which axes actually separate this market into groups that need different copy. The sharp angle comes from company fields more often than people assume.

- **Vertical or subcategory**, when value or language changes by vertical.
- **Company size or maturity.** At a ten-person company the owner is the operator; at larger firms persona and process language land. Size splits are campaigns.
- **Persona**, the decision-maker type; founder or owner status dominates functional title.
- **Company facts the rows carry**: platform, plan tier, tech stack, business model, revenue band, age, a rating the reader owns. Whichever changes what the first line can truthfully say.
- **Channel**, a named contact versus a public inbox. Different copy, different campaigns.
- **Geography**, usually personalisation, unless one geo stands alone.
- **Language**, a real axis in non-English markets; the contact's name, not the company's location, tells you.
- **Deliverability**, the one axis that is not about copy, below.

## The deliverability fence

People behind a secure email gateway split by where a send can safely go, not by what it says. The signal is the mail-provider column where the source carries it; mostly blank means skip the fence and say so. Three classes: sendable, gateway, dead (no mailbox at all, a guaranteed bounce).

The rule: in a bounce spike, stop sending to gateways entirely; in steady state, segregate them into their own campaign per segment, same copy, so their bounce behavior reads in isolation and only that campaign gets paused. Gateways are fenced, not excluded, and a fence never changes the copy; different copy for a gateway slice means a fence has been confused with an audience. Sendable is defined as NOT-gateway, never as an allow-list. Which providers are gateways is a standard, handed to you. A gateway slice under 2 to 5% is too thin to read; raise it rather than quietly cutting it.

## The brake, and its asymmetry

The drive is maximum specificity; the brake is volume. The grid of company axis against persona axis shows which cells stand and which merge. Some roles are structurally scarce per company, so a thin persona grows only by net-new companies, not by pulling deeper.

## Variants are not campaigns

Variants test angles against the same audience inside one campaign. Different copy to a different audience is a different campaign; reaching for variants with two audiences corrupts the read on what works.

## Priority

Segments overlap in reality: one row can qualify for two, and a row qualifying for none still has to land somewhere. Every set carries a second judgment: the priority order, which campaign deserves a row that qualifies for two. Usually the sharper argument wins the row. Made with the Operator, never defaulted. The set then deploys as a prioritized cascade with a catch-all at the bottom (the clayroots skill).

## How to show it

One real markdown table, rows in priority order:

| Priority | Segment | Axis | The reason (what the copy can now say) | Est. size | Gateway slice |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Under it, three labeled lines: **Overlaps:** where segments collide and who wins. **Folded:** the slices merged and why. **Open:** anything waiting on a call. Never create a field just to segment on: a split with no field to express it is a missing variable to raise, or a derived field to propose.
